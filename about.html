About

A project held by a life

THREAD brings archival research, sound and collaborative composition to bear on the history and present of gynaecological racism.

Anarcha

Where did she go?

Between 1845 and 1849, in Montgomery, Alabama, the surgeon J. Marion Sims performed repeated experimental operations on enslaved women. Anarcha was one of them. By Sims’s own account, she underwent around thirty operations.

She was later declared “cured”. We use that word deliberately and in quotation marks: there is little evidence about what her life was like afterwards, and almost nothing in her own voice.

THREAD begins from that silence. It does not try to fill it with certainty. It listens to it, composes with it, and asks what the archive’s gaps can teach us about how Black women’s bodies and accounts are still heard, or not heard, in care today.

The project

Two lanes, one thread

The work moves in two directions at once.

Back, into the archive, to speculate through composition and collective storytelling about Anarcha’s life after she was called “cured”.

Forward, into the present, where Black and Brown people still face disproportionate maternal and infant death, in the UK and globally. Here the project develops a research programme alongside the communities living this, using Endarkened Co-Composition, and their stories shape the second part of the opera.

Underneath both lanes is a question about method: can arts-based approaches be a way of doing clinical and social research, not only of responding to it?

The name

Why THREAD

Testimony, Healing, Repair, Embodied Archives, Dreaming.

The research is moving toward wholeness: reframing these histories rather than treating the people in them as broken things to be repaired. A thread is what runs through, connects, and holds.

THREAD is a working name.
